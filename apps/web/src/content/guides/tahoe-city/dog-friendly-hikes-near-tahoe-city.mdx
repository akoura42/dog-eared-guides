Burton Creek State Park begins about a mile behind downtown Tahoe City, and
California State Parks allows dogs there on fire roads only. Not on the
singletrack that crosses the same forest. The park page states it in four
words: "Dogs allowed only on fire roads." Six miles of unpaved roadway,
sunrise to sunset, and a dog's legal footprint stops at the edge of the
grade.

Drive four miles down the West Shore and the rule inverts. Ward Creek Park
Property is also State Parks, also Sierra District, and its page reads:
"Dogs allowed in developed areas only, which include campsites, paved roads
and paths, dirt fire roads, and paved parking areas. Dogs allowed in
developed picnic areas and day use areas. Dogs not allowed on beaches or
dirt trails." Same agency, same district, and the fire-road-yes /
dirt-trail-no line is drawn explicitly rather than left to inference.

Neither boundary is marked on the ground. That's the actual condition of
hiking here with a dog: four agencies inside a five-mile radius, each
publishing a different rule, and the transitions between them unsigned.

## The four rulebooks

- **Placer County.** Tahoe City is unincorporated. County code makes it
  unlawful to let an animal run at large, defined as off the owner's
  premises and not restrained by lead, leash, or adequate enclosure. This
  is the floor under everything else.
- **Tahoe City Public Utility District.** The 23-mile paved multi-use
  network. Its posted rules of the trail include "keep dogs on a short
  leash," a 15 mph speed limit, and bikes yielding to pedestrians.
- **California State Parks, Sierra District.** Statewide baseline: "Dogs
  must be on a maximum 6-foot leash at ALL times and physically under your
  control," and "do not take your dog (unless your dog is a service animal)
  into buildings or undeveloped areas, or on trails, unless specifically
  designated as being open to dogs on leash." Dogs must not be left
  unattended.
- **US Forest Service, Lake Tahoe Basin Management Unit.** The most
  permissive of the four: "Your well-behaved, leashed dog is welcome almost
  anywhere within the Lake Tahoe Basin Management Unit, with the notable
  exceptions of designated swimming beaches and areas that are restricted
  for wildlife protection. You must keep your dog on a leash, and pick up
  after them."
- **Tahoe National Forest.** Governs Granite Chief Wilderness, west of the
  ridge, with its own seasonal closure.

Read those side by side and the State Parks language is the outlier. On
national forest land the default is yes-with-a-leash. On state park land the
default is no, with exceptions granted per park.

## The paved network: the one that never changes

TCPUD's system is 23 miles across three legs radiating from the Y, and the
dog rule is identical on all of it. 64-Acres Park is the trailhead with
actual parking, which is scarce at Tahoe trailheads generally. The signage
in the lot carries two agency logos, Forest Service and TCPUD, because the
land underneath belongs to the LTBMU while the district runs the trail
through it. Both agencies require leashed dogs, so nothing changes at that
boundary even though it's the one place where a boundary is visible.

## National forest: where the default flips

The LTBMU rule is the reason most of the good walking around here works.
Leashed dogs are welcome almost anywhere in the unit, with two carve-outs.
The first is designated swimming beaches, and the agency names all six:
Nevada, Pope, Baldwin, Meeks Bay, William Kent, and Zephyr Cove. William
Kent sits just south of town on the West Shore, which makes it the one most
likely to be reached by accident from Tahoe City.

The second carve-out is areas restricted for wildlife protection, which is
where the Granite Chief closure lives.

Kaspian, four miles south on Highway 89, is a free day-use strip at the
mouth of Blackwood Canyon. Its own Forest Service page publishes no pet
policy at all; the basin-wide rule is what governs it, and Kaspian is not on
the list of six.

<VenueEmbed city="tahoe-city" slug="kaspian-day-use-area" />

Page Meadows sits above the West Shore off Pineland Drive, reached by
Forest Service roads 15N60 and 16N48. Two things are worth stating plainly
about it. It's LTBMU land, so the leashed-dog rule applies. And the Forest
Service's own description said there is no designated trail there — the
per-site page has since been retired and now redirects to the unit's
recreation index, so no current agency page describes conditions at Page
Meadows specifically.

## Granite Chief Wilderness and the fawning closure

West of the ridge, the land becomes Tahoe National Forest. Trailheads reach
it from Highway 89 via Alpine Meadows Road, Olympic Valley Road, Ward Creek
Boulevard, Blackwood Canyon Road, and Barker Pass Road. Wilderness permits
are not presently required for day or overnight use, and no fees are
presently required either. Group size is capped at 12 people.

The dog rule here is seasonal and geographically narrow. The Forest Service
states: "Dogs are prohibited from May 15 through July 15 each year in the
following deer fawning areas: the wilderness portion of the French Meadow
Game Refuge (except for the area east of the Pacific Crest Trail) and the
Big Springs Trail and the portion of Five Lakes Creek that parallels that
trail."

Two months, and only in those named places. The rest of the wilderness
keeps the leashed-dogs-welcome default through the same window. The
closure names Big Springs Trail and the stretch of Five Lakes Creek beside
it; the agency text doesn't state whether the Five Lakes Trail approach
from Alpine Meadows Road falls inside that description.

## The Tahoe Rim Trail

The TRT touches the Burton Creek area and runs the ridgeline above town.
The Tahoe Rim Trail Association's position is unambiguous: "Like most
trails in the Tahoe Basin, the Tahoe Rim Trail is dog-friendly." No section
is listed as closed to dogs.

Its guidance is more specific than most. Dogs leashed around other trail
users, at trailheads, and on steep or slippery terrain, and under voice
control at all times. A 1:1 dog-to-human ratio, because dogs outnumbering
people are harder to control quickly. Waste packed out on day hikes. And a
line that reads like it came from experience: pack separate water for the
dog, because long segments have no reliable water source.

That last point compounds with a Forest Service warning that giardia is
present in Tahoe-area lakes and streams, and with California's harmful
algal bloom advisories, which have been posted around Lake Tahoe in warm
months and which can sicken a dog within hours. The state maintains a map
of current HAB advisories.

## November 1 to May 1

A Sierra District superintendent's order signed May 5, 2022 prohibits dogs
on designated ski trails within the district from November 1 to May 1
annually, when snow is present. The stated reason is protecting the
integrity of the groomed Nordic courses. Burton Creek State Park and Ed
Z'berg Sugar Pine Point State Park are both named in the order, along with
Donner Memorial, South Yuba River, Empire Mine, Emerald Bay, and
Plumas-Eureka. The order exempts trained service dogs.

Burton Creek is the one that bites locally. The fire roads that are the
only dog-legal route through the park in summer are the same ground that
gets groomed in winter. From November 1, with snow down, that access closes.

The same order works the other direction at the Truckee River outlet: it
lists Gatekeepers as a day-use area where leashed dogs are permitted, which
is why that spot is the exception inside Tahoe State Recreation Area.

What stays walkable through winter is the pavement. TCPUD clears snow from
16 of the network's 23 miles, starting when snowfall reaches three inches
and proceeding in priority order as staff capacity and conditions allow.
Sixteen plowed miles is the town's most dependable snow-season dog walk.

For groomed snow specifically, two operations publish dog policies rather
than banning them.

## The off-leash exception

Everything above assumes a leash, because every one of the four agencies
requires one. The fenced TCPUD dog park at 375 Grove Street is the
designated off-leash spot in town.

<VenueEmbed city="tahoe-city" slug="tahoe-city-dog-park" />

One more piece of local mechanics: dogs can't ride the TART bus. The rider
rules state that "animals, other than service animals, are not permitted on
buses," and the free TART Connect shuttle holds the same line in the North
Lake Tahoe zone. The separate Truckee TART Connect zone allows well-behaved
pets in crates. Every trailhead in this guide is therefore a
drive-or-walk proposition from town, and the 64-Acres lot is the largest of
them.
